A.  CALIBRATION AND THE eGFR-POTASSIUM CURVE
==============================================================================

  Three parameters (S0, adapt_p, adapt_max) were fitted to three anchors
  (eGFR 100, 20 and 12).  The other three rows are PREDICTIONS, not fits.

   eGFR   K_pred  K_target   err    FE_K%   U_K    colon   aldo
  ------ ------- --------- ------ ------- ------ ------- -------
     100    4.20     4.20   -0.00*   10.3   62.0    10.0    12.0
      60    4.47     4.40   +0.07    14.7   56.6    15.4    17.0
      45    4.61     4.55   +0.06    18.2   54.3    17.7    20.4
      30    4.80     4.75   +0.05    24.8   51.5    20.5    26.3
      20    5.00     5.00   -0.00*   34.2   49.3    22.7    34.0
      12    5.30     5.30   -0.00*   51.2   46.9    25.1    50.1
  ( * = anchor used for calibration )
  mean |error| over the 4 held-out eGFRs = 0.059 mmol/L

  READ THIS OFF THE FE_K COLUMN, IT IS THE WHOLE DISEASE:  the fraction
  of filtered K excreted rises from 10% to 51% as eGFR falls from
  100 to 12.  The kidney does not fail at potassium gradually -- it
  spends its entire reserve keeping serum K flat, and serum K only
  moves once that reserve is gone.  Serum K is therefore a LATE marker
  of a reserve that has been depleting for years.

B.  THE KALIURESIS RESERVE, AND WHAT EACH DRUG COSTS OF IT
==============================================================================
  Steady-state serum K (mmol/L) vs eGFR under four regimens, diet 80 mmol/day

   eGFR    none    ACEi   ACEi+MRA   ACEi+MRA+patiromer 16.8 g
  ------ ------- ------- ---------- --------------------------
      90    4.26    4.49     4.78        4.16
      75    4.35    4.58     4.88        4.23
      60    4.47    4.70     5.00        4.31
      45    4.61    4.84     5.14        4.40
      35    4.73    4.96     5.26        4.47
      25    4.89    5.12     5.41        4.55
      20    5.00    5.22     5.51        4.60
      15    5.14    5.36     5.64        4.66
      12    5.30    5.51     5.78        4.72

  eGFR at which steady-state K crosses 5.5 mmol/L:
     no RAAS blockade ..................  11.3 mL/min/1.73
     + ACE inhibitor ...................  12.0
     + ACEi and MRA ....................  20.6   (+9.3 vs untreated)
     + ACEi, MRA and patiromer .........   6.7

  THE POINT:  adding an MRA does not raise K by a fixed amount, it moves
  the eGFR at which the patient becomes hyperkalaemic UP by 9 mL/min.
  A patient at eGFR 21 is not 'a bit hyperkalaemic on spironolactone';
  they have been converted into the renal-function equivalent of eGFR 11.
  A binder moves the same threshold back down to 6.7, i.e. it buys back
  14 mL/min/1.73 of *apparent* potassium-handling capacity without
  changing a single nephron.

C.  THE DIETARY CEILING -- AND WHY A BINDER CANNOT OUTRUN A DIET
==============================================================================
  Maximum tolerable dietary K (mmol/day) holding steady-state K at 5.5:

   eGFR    none    ACEi+MRA   ACEi+MRA + SZC 10 g/d
  ------ ------- ----------- -----------------------
      60     105         95             133
      45     100         90             127
      30      94         84             120
      20      89         80             115
      15      86         77             111

  A binder's ceiling is structural: it can only capture potassium that is
  IN the lumen, so its maximum possible negative balance is bounded by
  phi_max * f_abs * intake.  At the calibrated phi_max this is:
     SZC 5 g/d            phi = 0.173  ->  max 12.5 mmol/day at 80 mmol/day intake
     SZC 10 g/d           phi = 0.250  ->  max 18.0 mmol/day at 80 mmol/day intake
     patiromer 8.4 g/d    phi = 0.173  ->  max 12.5 mmol/day at 80 mmol/day intake
     patiromer 16.8 g/d   phi = 0.245  ->  max 17.6 mmol/day at 80 mmol/day intake
     patiromer 25.2 g/d   phi = 0.285  ->  max 20.5 mmol/day at 80 mmol/day intake

  So no binder at any dose can remove more than ~43 mmol/day on a
  standard diet.  In a patient whose renal + colonic excretion has fallen
  below intake by more than that, the binder is arithmetically incapable
  of restoring balance and dialysis is not a preference but a mass-balance
  requirement.

     virtual patient: eGFR 16.5, K 5.60

     SZC 10.0 g/day -> K 4.63  (trial 4.5, err +0.13)
     SZC 15.0 g/day -> K 4.49  (trial 4.4, err +0.09)

  MRA potassium cost, model vs trials:
     RALES spironolactone 25 mg (eGFR ~60)      +0.30   (trial +0.30)
     FIDELIO finerenone 20 mg (eGFR ~44)        +0.10   (trial +0.23)
     spironolactone 25 mg in CKD 4 (eGFR 25)    +0.30   (no RCT (excluded from trials))


  The finerenone row is a DISCREPANCY, not an agreement: assuming a MR load
  35% of spironolactone 25 mg under-predicts the FIDELIO potassium signal.
  Back-solving, FIDELIO's +0.23 mmol/L requires a MR load 72% of
  spironolactone 25 mg -- i.e. finerenone at 20 mg is NOT the weak MR
  blocker a 35% assumption implies.  The model cannot distinguish 'more
  occupancy' from 'same occupancy, different tissue distribution', which is
  exactly the claim made for non-steroidal MRAs, so this is reported as an
  unresolved identifiability problem rather than a fitted parameter.

E.  ACUTE HYPERKALAEMIA -- TWO PATIENTS WITH THE SAME NUMBER
==============================================================================
  Serum potassium is a RATIO of a pool to a partition,
        Ce  such that  K_total = Ce*V_ecf + Ci0*LAMrel*(Ce/Ce0)^alpha*V_icf,
  so the same laboratory number can mean opposite things about the pool.

  FIRST, THE EXCHANGE RATE.  How many mmol of whole-body potassium is one
  mmol/L of serum potassium worth?  The model answers this WITHOUT being
  told, from alpha alone:
     fully equilibrated (chronic) ... 224 mmol per mmol/L
     fast pool only (minutes) ....... 66 mmol per mmol/L
  The chronic figure is a PREDICTION and can be checked: the classical
  deficit nomogram says a stable serum K of 3.0 implies a whole-body
  deficit of roughly 200-400 mmol.  This model, never having been shown
  that number, gives:
     serum K 3.5 -> whole-body deficit 168 mmol
     serum K 3.0 -> whole-body deficit 302 mmol
     serum K 2.5 -> whole-body deficit 454 mmol
     serum K 2.0 -> whole-body deficit 629 mmol
  The acute figure is separately checkable: an IV load of ~66 mmol given
  faster than muscle can take it up raises serum K by 1 mmol/L, which is
  why 40 mmol of KCl pushed too quickly is dangerous and 40 mmol eaten is
  not.  Neither number was fitted; both fall out of alpha = 0.25.

  PATIENT A -- mass-balance hyperkalaemia.  eGFR 12, ACEi + spironolactone,
  HCO3 19, glucose normal, serum K 6.80.  LAMrel = 0.999 (normal).
     K_total ........................ 4069 mmol
     surfeit over a normal adult .... +482 mmol
     An AKI halving eGFR to 6 with the diet up at 120 mmol/day opens an
     imbalance of +58 mmol/day, so that surfeit takes 8 days to build.

  PATIENT B -- partition hyperkalaemia.  Diabetic ketoacidosis: HCO3 5
  (pH 7.09), glucose 40, insulin 0.5 uU/mL, and the 400 mmol osmotic-
  diuresis deficit that DKA always carries.
     LAMrel collapses to ............ 0.855 (85% of normal)
     K_total ........................ 3187 mmol  (-400 vs normal)
     PRESENTING serum K ............. 4.82   -- reassuringly normal
     serum K once insulin and alkali restore LAMrel to 1.0, with NO
     potassium given ................ 2.67   -- and that is the arrest

  Patient A is +482 mmol above normal and reads 6.80.  Patient B is -400
  mmol BELOW normal and reads 4.82.  The number does not carry the
  information; only the number TOGETHER WITH LAMrel does.  A binder is the
  only therapy that helps A.  For B a binder is actively harmful, and the
  therapy that 'works' -- insulin -- is precisely what uncovers the deficit.

  Rescue therapies applied to PATIENT A (still eating, 80 mmol/day):

  arm                              0h     1h     2h     4h     8h    24h    48h   K removed

  no treatment                   6.74   6.73   6.71   6.70   6.69   6.63   6.55        0.0 mmol
  calcium gluconate 1 g          6.74   6.73   6.71   6.70   6.69   6.63   6.55        0.0 mmol
  insulin 10 U + D50 25 g        5.96   5.81   6.33   6.68   6.68   6.63   6.55        0.0 mmol

  The insulin arm falls to 5.81 at 45 min, is back to 6.68 by 4 h and by
  48 h is at 6.55 -- INDISTINGUISHABLE from the untreated arm's 6.55,
  because it removed 0.0 mmol.  The SZC arm is slower at 4 h (6.59 vs 6.68)
  but reaches 5.85 at 48 h having removed 69 mmol, and that is the only
  fall of the two that does not come back.

  DISCREPANCY, stated rather than tuned away: HARMONIZE reports roughly
  -1.1 mmol/L at 48 h on SZC 10 g TID from a baseline of 5.6; this model
  gives -0.95 from 6.80.  The model is slower early.  The most likely
  structural cause is that a single fast pool plus a single slow pool is
  still too coarse a description of muscle, and the trial patients' intake
  fell when they were admitted while this simulation keeps them eating
  80 mmol/day throughout.

  How much dextrose does 10 U of insulin need?  (K nadir vs glucose nadir)

  The model does NOT reproduce the ~15-20% incidence of frank hypoglycaemia
  reported after insulin/dextrose for hyperkalaemia: even with no dextrose
  at all its nadir stays above 4 mmol/L.  Two things are missing and both
  are structural -- the insulin effect site here decays with the plasma
  level (real tissue action outlasts it by hours), and there is no
  between-patient variation in glycogen reserve, which is what actually
  separates the patient who becomes hypoglycaemic from the one who does
  not.  This is a place the model should not be trusted.

F.  THE RAASi-POTASSIUM DILEMMA, SIMULATED WITH A CLINICIAN IN THE LOOP
==============================================================================
  A clinician is modelled as a controller on the prescribed RAASi dose:
     dRD/dt = -k_down*max(0, K-5.5)*RD + k_up*max(0, 5.0-K)*(1-RD)
  i.e. the dose comes down when K breaches 5.5 and creeps back up when it
  is safe.  Nothing about the outcome below is imposed; it is what the
  controller does when it is coupled to the potassium physiology.

  5-year outcome of a diabetic CKD 4 patient (eGFR 25 at entry, diet 80):

  arm                                            K_5y  RAASi eGFR_5y   d>5.5  meanHR
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
  ACEi + MRA, no binder (clinician titrates)     5.50    51%    14.1    1009   0.861
  ACEi + MRA + patiromer 16.8 g/d                4.66   100%    15.0       0   0.639
  ACEi + MRA + SZC 10 g/d                        4.65   100%    15.0       0   0.638
  ACEi + MRA, low-K diet 50 mmol/d               4.47   100%    15.0       0   0.638
  ACEi + MRA + furosemide 40 mg/d                5.50    98%    15.0      44   0.788
  ACEi + MRA + SGLT2i                            5.48   100%    15.0       0   0.777
  ACEi + MRA + NaHCO3 70 mmol/d                  5.50    98%    15.0      24   0.788
  ACEi alone, MRA never started                  5.48   100%    12.0       0   0.891

  Read the RAASi column, not the K column.  Every arm ends up with an
  acceptable potassium -- that is what the controller is for.  What differs
  is the PRICE PAID IN RAASi DOSE to get there:
     ACEi + MRA + patiromer 16.8 g/d              RAASi 100% vs  51%, eGFR +0.9 mL/min at 5 y
     ACEi + MRA + SZC 10 g/d                      RAASi 100% vs  51%, eGFR +0.9 mL/min at 5 y
     ACEi + MRA, low-K diet 50 mmol/d             RAASi 100% vs  51%, eGFR +0.9 mL/min at 5 y

  And the mean hazard column is the composite the patient actually
  experiences: potassium risk multiplied by the benefit forgone.
     lowest mean hazard: ACEi + MRA, low-K diet 50 mmol/d (0.638)
     vs no-binder titration: 0.861, a 25.9% relative reduction

G.  VIRTUAL POPULATION -- WHO ACTUALLY BENEFITS FROM A BINDER
==============================================================================
  600 virtual patients on a deterministic lattice over the three things
  that actually vary between real patients and are cheap to measure:
     eGFR 15-60, dietary K 40-140 mmol/day, serum HCO3 16-26 mmol/L.

  n = 600.  On ACEi+MRA without a binder, 315 (52.5%) sit above K 5.5.
  Adding patiromer 16.8 g/day brings 194 of those 315 (61.6%) back under 5.5.
  121 patients (20.2%) remain above 5.5 despite the binder.

   eGFR band     n    %K>5.5 no binder    %K>5.5 with binder
  ----------- ----- ------------------- --------------------
   15-29        180          57.2%               26.7%
   30-44        180          53.3%               20.6%
   45-59        180          48.9%               15.6%
   60-74         60          46.7%               13.3%

  The binder's benefit is not uniform.  It is concentrated where the
  patient is above threshold BY LESS THAN THE BINDER'S CEILING, and that
  is a computable band, not a clinical impression.

H.  DISCREPANCIES AND WHAT THE MODEL CANNOT DO
==============================================================================
  (1) BICARBONATE ACTS ON THE KIDNEY, NOT ON THE CELL.  Building the model
      from first principles produced a result I did not put in by hand:
      the transcellular route contributes almost nothing chronically,
      because at steady state serum K is pinned by MASS BALANCE and the
      partition only decides how big the body pool behind it is.
      HCO3 16.0 -> pH 7.344, LAMrel 0.994, U_K 48.1, steady-state K 5.44
      HCO3 18.0 -> pH 7.362, LAMrel 0.997, U_K 48.7, steady-state K 5.31
      HCO3 20.0 -> pH 7.377, LAMrel 0.999, U_K 49.3, steady-state K 5.20
      HCO3 22.0 -> pH 7.390, LAMrel 1.001, U_K 49.8, steady-state K 5.11
      HCO3 24.0 -> pH 7.401, LAMrel 1.003, U_K 50.2, steady-state K 5.03
      Correcting HCO3 18 -> 24 is worth -0.28 mmol/L, and 100% of that
      comes from the +1.5 mmol/day of extra KALIURESIS, not from a shift.
      The corollary is the well-documented negative result: bicarbonate is
      NOT an acute hyperkalaemia therapy, because the mechanism it actually
      has needs a kidney and needs weeks.

  (2) THE MRA POTASSIUM COST IS FLAT, WHICH CONTRADICTS WHAT I EXPECTED.
      I built this model expecting the MRA penalty to grow as eGFR fell.
      It does not: the model gives +0.30, +0.30, +0.30, +0.29, +0.28 across
      eGFR 90 to 15.  What grows is not the increment but the BASELINE it
      is added to, so the same +0.30 tips a rising fraction of patients over
      5.5.  That is a different clinical claim -- it says the danger is not
      a steeper drug effect but a shorter distance to the threshold -- and
      it is the version supported by the model rather than by my prior.

  (3) NOT REPRESENTED:  acute kidney injury superimposed on CKD (the single
      commonest real-world cause of severe hyperkalaemia), digoxin toxicity,
      trimethoprim/heparin/calcineurin-inhibitor effects on the ASDN, tumour
      lysis and rhabdomyolysis (K released from the ICF pool itself, which
      this model treats as a passive reservoir), pseudohyperkalaemia, and
      dialysis (an intermittent, extremely high-clearance removal term).

  (4) THE OUTCOME LAYER IS ASSOCIATIVE, NOT CAUSAL.  hazard_K() is fitted to
      OBSERVATIONAL K-mortality curves, in which a high potassium is partly
      a marker of the illness that caused it.  The model therefore almost
      certainly OVERSTATES the benefit of lowering a number and understates
      the benefit of treating what raised it.  Every hazard figure in
      section F should be read with that bias in mind: it is the strongest
      assumption in the whole model and the least well supported.

  (5) The intracellular space is TWO pools, which is enough to reproduce the
      chronic (224 mmol per mmol/L) and acute (66 mmol per mmol/L) buffer
      capacities simultaneously, but it is still not muscle.  Exercise, which
      raises venous K by >1 mmol/L within a minute through interstitial
      accumulation in the working limb, is not representable at all; nor is
      the release of potassium FROM the pool in rhabdomyolysis and tumour
      lysis, which this model treats only as a passive reservoir.

  (6) The clinician controller in section F is a caricature: real
      down-titration is discrete, delayed by the interval between blood
      tests, and frequently permanent.  A model in which the dose creeps
      back up automatically is optimistic about exactly the behaviour the
      binder trials were designed to change.
