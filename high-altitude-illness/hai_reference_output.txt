2.  WHY VENTILATION BUYS MORE AT ALTITUDE -- and it is not the mmHg
==============================================================================
 alt(m)   PaCO2  dPAO2/dVA  dSaO2/dPaO2   dSaO2/dVA  ratio vs SL
      0    40.3      10.09        0.081        0.82         1.00
   2500    34.3       7.32        0.267        1.96         2.38
   3500    30.9       5.93        0.423        2.51         3.05
   4559    26.9       4.51        0.669        3.02         3.67
   5300    24.1       3.62        0.902        3.26         3.97
   6400    20.7       2.65        1.408        3.73         4.54
   8000    16.8       1.76        2.407        4.23         5.15
   8848    15.1       1.42        2.844        4.05         4.93

  dPAO2/dVA = c*PaCO2/VA contains NO barometric pressure -- PB cancels out
  of the derivative entirely.  What a litre of ventilation is worth in mmHg
  depends only on where you already sit on the CO2 hyperbola, and by that
  measure hyperventilation gets STEADILY WORSE with altitude: 10.1 mmHg per
  L/min at sea level, 1.4 on the summit, because you are already breathing.
  It is the DISSOCIATION CURVE that reverses the verdict: dSaO2/dPaO2 rises
  35-fold over the same range, the product rises ~5-fold, and the entire
  value of hyperventilating at altitude therefore lives in the SIGMOID and
  not in the gas equation.  A drug that raised PaO2 by 5 mmHg would be worth
  0.4 saturation points at sea level and 12 points at 8000 m.

4.  HAPE: THE CEILING IS SET BY HETEROGENEITY, NOT BY HPV STRENGTH
==============================================================================
  Pcap,open = P_LA + Q * (r_v/mu) / [ a/lambda + (1-a)/mu ]

  Take lambda -> infinity.  If the "non-responsive" bed truly does not
  constrict (kappa = 0, mu = 1) the flow term tends to Q*r_v/(1-a) and the
  amplification ceiling is 1/(1-a).  That is the clean statement, and it is
  the WRONG one for a real lung: with kappa > 0, mu = 1 + kappa(lambda-1)
  diverges along with lambda, and the correct limit is

        amplification ceiling = 1 / [ 1 - a(1 - kappa) ]

  At kappa = 0.08 the naive formula overstates the ceiling by 45% at a = 0.85.
  Either way the conclusion stands and is the point of the submodel:
  HETEROGENEITY sets the ceiling, HPV strength only sets how fast you
  reach it.  A drug that halves lambda moves you along a curve that has
  already flattened; a lung with a = 0.5 cannot get there at all.

5.  A HYPOTHESIS THE MODEL REFUTES: the P50 "paradox"
==============================================================================
 alt(m)     pH    DPG    P50  shift(pH)  shift(DPG)   SaO2%
      0  7.401   5.03  26.77      -0.04        0.01    97.3
   2500  7.421   5.23  26.29      -0.60        0.09    93.4
   3500  7.432   5.37  26.00      -0.94        0.14    90.6
   4559  7.447   5.58  25.68      -1.35        0.22    86.4
   5300  7.457   5.77  25.45      -1.64        0.29    82.7
   6400  7.471   6.00  25.15      -2.02        0.37    74.6
   8000  7.487   6.00  24.70      -2.47        0.37    57.1
   8848  7.495   6.00  24.50      -2.67        0.36    45.6

  HYPOTHESIS UNDER TEST: two shifts oppose each other -- 2,3-DPG moves the
  curve RIGHT (helps unloading at the muscle), respiratory alkalosis moves
  it LEFT (helps loading at the lung).  The textbook story is that DPG wins
  at moderate altitude and alkalosis wins at extreme altitude, so P50 should
  rise, PEAK somewhere around 4000-5000 m, and then fall.

  REFUTED.  With a 2,3-DPG rise calibrated to the measured ~20% (5.0 -> 6.0 mmol/L),
  P50 is monotonically DECREASING at every altitude: 26.77 mmHg at sea level to 24.50 on the summit.
  The reason is arithmetic and one-sided: the DPG term contributes at most +0.37 mmHg because it SATURATES,
  while the alkalosis term reaches -2.67 mmHg and does not.  There is no crossing point to find.
  A peak could only exist if 2,3-DPG rose by ~50%, which it does not.
  The surviving -- and stronger -- claim is about MAGNITUDE, not shape:
  Counterfactual: with the SAME PaO2 (23.0 mmHg) but no
  respiratory alkalosis, summit SaO2 would be 39.0% instead of 45.6% -- the alkalosis is worth 6.6 saturation points.

6.  OPTIMAL HAEMATOCRIT -- exact, and it does not move with altitude
==============================================================================
  DO2 ~ Hct * exp(-k*gamma*Hct)  =>  Hct* = 1/(k*gamma),  k = 2.31
  gamma     Hct*   (gamma = exponent of the cardiac-output penalty Q ~ mu^-gamma)
   1.00    0.433
   0.90    0.481
   0.80    0.541
   0.75    0.577
   0.60    0.722
   0.50    0.866

  SaO2 CANCELS out of the optimisation -- CaO2 is proportional to Hct
  whatever the saturation.  So the model asserts the optimum is 43.3% at
  sea level and 43.3% at 5000 m.  To justify an Andean resident's Hct of 55%
  the cardiac-output penalty exponent would have to be gamma <= 0.787, i.e. the
  circulation would have to absorb most of the viscosity cost.  That is the
  quantitative form of the Tibetan-vs-Andean argument.

==============================================================================
arm                           SaO2 min   PaCO2   HCO3  LLS peak   AMS-h

  Acetazolamide moves SaO2 by +0.8 points and LLS by -0.96.
  Dexamethasone moves SaO2 by -0.1 points and LLS by -0.94.
  Two drugs, comparable symptomatic benefit, and only one of them has
  changed the patient's oxygen.  Every guideline that says "descend if
  symptoms persist" is implicitly using the symptom as an oxygen gauge --
  and dexamethasone is the drug that breaks the gauge.

11.  HAPE BIFURCATION -- the critical altitude for a phenotype
==============================================================================

  normal, 3x VO2               critical altitude = 4673 m
  HAPE-susceptible, rest       critical altitude = none below 7000 m
  HAPE-susceptible, 3x VO2     critical altitude = 2000 m

  Exercise moves the critical altitude by more than a thousand metres in
  the susceptible phenotype and by more than that in the normal one.  The
  bifurcation parameter that clinicians can actually control is not the
  altitude.
